ATS-Friendly Resume Format: What Actually Parses

File type, columns, fonts and headings — the layout choices that decide whether an ATS can read your resume at all

September 7, 2026 Patrick Galyen

Most “my resume gets no responses” problems are not about your experience — they’re about format. An Applicant Tracking System has to parse your document into fields, and the wrong layout garbles it before a recruiter ever searches. Here is the format that actually parses cleanly.

File type: .docx beats a design-tool PDF

A Word .docx is the safest, most universally parseable format. PDFs can parse well — but only if the text is real, selectable text. A PDF exported from a design tool (Canva, InDesign) where the resume is effectively an image will parse as nothing. If you must send PDF, make sure you can select and copy the text.

One column. Always.

Multi-column layouts, tables, and text boxes are the number-one parsing killer. The parser reads left-to-right, top-to-bottom; a two-column design interleaves your sidebar into your job history and turns it to nonsense. Use a single column, top to bottom.

Headings, fonts, and the small stuff

  • Standard section headings: “Work Experience,” “Education,” “Skills.” Creative headings (“Where I’ve Made Impact”) can miss the parser’s expected buckets.
  • Common fonts: Arial, Calibri, Georgia, Times. Nothing exotic.
  • No headers/footers for contact info — many parsers ignore that region. Put your name, email and phone in the body.
  • No graphics, logos, icons or photos. They carry no parseable text and can break layout.
  • Consistent dates: one format, e.g. Jan 2023 – Mar 2025, everywhere.

What actually happens to your file

It helps to know what you’re formatting for. When your resume lands, the system runs three steps in order, and each one can quietly fail. First it extracts raw text from the file — a .docx exposes a clean text layer, while a PDF exported from a design tool may store text as positioned glyphs or outlines, which extract as gibberish or nothing. Second it segments the text into sections, looking for conventional headings to decide where Experience ends and Education begins. Third it pulls structured entities out of those sections: employer, title, dates, school, skills.

Nearly every “the ATS ate my resume” story is step two or three going wrong. Your creative section heading — “Where I’ve Made an Impact” — isn’t recognized, so the parser never identifies an Experience section, and a recruiter filtering by years of experience never sees you. The content was perfect. The label wasn’t.

The traps that silently break parsing

These are the specific constructs that survive a visual check and fail a machine read:

  • Tables. Parsers commonly read a table row-first, so a two-column layout interleaves unrelated text: your job title merges with a date from the other column.
  • Text boxes and headers/footers. Content placed in these often sits outside the main document flow and is skipped entirely. Never put your phone number or email in a header.
  • Graphics for meaning. Skill bars, rating dots and icon-only contact details carry zero text. “Python ●●●●○” extracts as “Python” at best.
  • Exotic bullets and ligatures. Decorative glyphs can extract as replacement characters, splitting sentences mid-word.
  • Ambiguous dates. “’21–’23” is far more likely to be misread than “January 2021 – March 2023”. Spell out the month and use a full year.

The 60-second self-test

You don’t need special software to check any of this. Open your resume, select all, copy, and paste it into a plain text editor. What you see is approximately what the parser sees. If the reading order scrambles, if columns interleave, if whole blocks vanish — that is the failure, and it will happen the same way on their end. Fix it until the plain-text version reads top to bottom in the order you intended.

The file name is part of the submission

A small thing that costs nothing and is worth doing properly: the file name usually survives into the recruiter's system, and often into the list view where they triage candidates.

Resume_final_v3_USE THIS ONE.docx is the version they see. So is resume.docx, repeated forty times in a folder where every file is called the same thing. Jane-Okafor-Resume.docx is trivially better, and in a system that stores uploads by name it is the difference between being findable and being a duplicate.

Avoid characters beyond letters, numbers, hyphens and underscores. Spaces are usually fine but occasionally get mangled into escape sequences by older portals, and a file arriving as Jane%20Okafor.docx is a needlessly bad first impression.

Test it before you trust it

The fastest sanity check: copy all the text out of your resume and paste it into a plain notepad. If the order is scrambled or fields are missing, the ATS sees that same mess. Then check the actual content against each posting — the right format gets you parsed; the right keywords get you ranked.
