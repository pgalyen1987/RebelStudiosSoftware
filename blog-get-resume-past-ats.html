How to Get Your Resume Past an ATS

What Applicant Tracking Systems actually do, the two real reasons resumes fail, and the mechanical fixes that work

September 7, 2026 Patrick Galyen

If your applications keep vanishing into a black hole, the culprit is usually an Applicant Tracking System (ATS) — the software almost every mid-size and large employer uses to receive, parse, and rank resumes before a human sees them. The good news: getting past it is mechanical, not mysterious. Here is what actually works in 2026.

What an ATS really does (and doesn’t)

First, kill the biggest myth: a modern ATS does not usually auto-reject resumes on its own. What it does is parse your resume into structured fields (name, work history, skills, dates) and make you searchable and rankable for the recruiter. You get filtered out when your resume either parses badly (so your experience lands in the wrong fields or gets lost) or doesn’t match the keywords the recruiter searches for. Both are fixable.

The two real reasons resumes fail

  • Parsing failure. Fancy formatting — columns, tables, text boxes, headers/footers, graphics — confuses the parser, so your data is garbled or dropped. See the format that actually parses.
  • Keyword mismatch. The recruiter searches for the skills in the job description; if your resume uses different words for the same thing, you don’t surface. See how ATS keyword matching works.

How to get past it, step by step

1. Mirror the job posting’s language. If the posting says “CI/CD,” don’t only write “build pipelines.” Use the posting’s exact terms where they’re truthfully yours — that’s what the recruiter searches.

2. Use a clean, single-column format. Standard section headings (“Experience,” “Education,” “Skills”), a common font, dates in a consistent format, no graphics.

3. Quantify. Recruiters and the humans behind them respond to numbers: “cut build times 40%,” “handled 2M requests/day.”

4. Match before you send. Compare your resume against each specific posting and fix the missing keywords — the single highest-leverage step, and the one people skip because it’s tedious to do by hand.

How the keyword match actually works

The mental model that trips people up is imagining something clever on the other end. Most keyword matching is far more literal than that: a recruiter searches or filters for specific terms, and your resume either contains those strings or it doesn’t. Semantic understanding is improving, but you should write for the dumb version, because the dumb version is what’s often deployed.

That produces one concrete tactic worth more than every “ATS hack” you’ll read: mirror the posting’s exact wording, and include both the acronym and its expansion. If the listing says “Search Engine Optimization (SEO)”, put both forms on the page — a filter for “SEO” will not match a resume that only says “Search Engine Optimization”. Same for “RN” and “Registered Nurse”, “CPA”, “AWS”, “K-12”. If they call it “client relations” and your resume says “customer success”, you are invisible to that search despite having done the job.

A tailoring workflow that doesn’t take an hour

“Tailor every application” is good advice that people abandon because it sounds like rewriting from scratch each time. It isn’t. Keep one master resume containing every bullet you might ever use — long, ugly, never sent. For each application, copy it and delete down to the most relevant version, then adjust the top third to echo the posting’s language. Deleting is dramatically faster than writing, and the top third is what decides everything.

Spend your effort there: the professional summary and the most recent role. That’s where both the filter and the human land first, and where a five-minute edit changes the outcome.

The ATS is not the last gate

Worth keeping in proportion — clearing the software only earns you the next few seconds. A human then skims, and skimming rewards different things than parsing does: scannable bullets that lead with an outcome, numbers that give scale, and no wall of text. Optimizing so hard for the machine that you produce something no person wants to read is its own way of losing.

What NOT to do

Don’t stuff invisible white-text keywords or cram the whole job description into your resume — recruiters see the parsed text and it reads as spam. Don’t use a PDF exported from a design tool with the text as an image. And don’t use a single generic resume for every job; the keyword match is per-posting.
